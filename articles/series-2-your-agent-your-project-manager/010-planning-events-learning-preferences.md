# Article 010 — Planning Events, Learning Preferences

## The story

- **One skill, first built for a dinner.** The event-planning skill started with a single job: a hosted dinner for a group I lead. It held the shape of a good evening — the timing, the room, the food, what to prepare — and the habit of checking the details twice. It worked, so it stayed.
- **Then it became the skill for every event.** The same skill was extended to all personal events. An evening out ran on it. A mountain day trip ran on it. The skill did not get bigger for each new kind of event; the same shape held, and each event taught it one more thing.
- **The debrief is the lesson.** The day after the evening out, I debriefed it: an optional stop skipped without stress — flexibility by design, not a failure of the plan — and a paid parking lot chosen when free street parking sat closer, because close mattered more than free that night. Both observations went into the skill the same day. A preference the agent only remembers is a guess next time; a preference written into the skill is a rule.
- **A week later, a new standing rule.** The mountain day trip produced one: on weekends, a business event and a date both outrank the day's routine. The agent's job is to surface the day's other commitments and ask before clearing any of them — and to watch for overcommitment before it happens, proposing the boundary while there is still time to choose.
- **The agent holds the plan so the person can stay loose inside it.** A good plan is not a tight plan. The plan's job is to carry the timing, the bookings, and the trade-offs, so the people in it can change their minds without the day falling apart.

## In plain words

- **Debrief** — a short review after an event: what worked, what flexed, what to change — written into the standing instructions the same day, while it is still fresh.
- **Standing rule** — a rule written into the agent's instructions that applies every time, until it is changed. A standing rule is how a one-time lesson becomes permanent behavior.
- **Boundary** — a line the agent proposes before the person crosses it: it surfaces a conflict, names what would have to give, and asks — instead of clearing the calendar on its own.

Every term also lives in the [glossary](../../glossary.md).

## How it works

The shape is: plan → flex → debrief → rule.

```text
Before: one skill, the shape of the event
│
├── the plan — timing, bookings, trade-offs, held by the agent
├── the flex — optional stops, changed minds, without stress
├── the debrief — what worked, what flexed, what to change
└── the rule — the debrief written into the skill, same day
```

Preferences compound when every event ends in a debrief that edits the playbook. The agent's job is not the perfect plan; it is a plan that flexes, boundaries proposed before burnout, and feedback that becomes a standing rule within a day. And asking before removing anything is what keeps the human the author of their own calendar: the agent proposes, the person disposes.

## Try it yourself

Plan one event the debrief way, the smallest version:

1. **Write the skill after the first event, not before.** Plan one real event, then write down what the plan actually was — the shape, not the details. A skill written before its first use is a guess.
2. **Name the optional parts.** Mark which stops, items, and times are optional before the day starts. An optional stop skipped without stress is flexibility by design; an unmarked one skipped feels like failure.
3. **Debrief the next day, in writing.** Three questions: what worked, what flexed, what would I change. Ten minutes, same day, while it is fresh.
4. **Fold one thing into the skill.** Not the whole debrief — the one change that would have helped most. A skill that grows by one rule per event stays small and gets smarter.
5. **Let the agent watch for overload.** Give it the standing rule: when a day holds more than it should, say so before the day starts, and propose what could give. A boundary proposed early is a choice; one discovered late is a cancellation.
6. **Never let it move or remove anything on its own.** The agent surfaces the conflict and asks. The calendar is the person's; the agent is the one who reads it carefully.

## What it proved

Three patterns from [patterns.md](../../patterns.md):

- **Pattern 1 — Context is infrastructure you own.** Preferences compound because they are written into the skill — files the owner controls — instead of living in conversation history that fades. A debrief that becomes a standing rule is context doing its job.
- **Pattern 4 — The human keeps the irreversible decisions.** The agent surfaces a conflict and asks before clearing anything. The calendar stays the person's because the removals stay theirs.
- **Pattern 5 — Minimum viable complexity.** One skill, one shape, one new rule per event. The plan got better by getting one rule at a time, not by growing a bigger system.

And one thing that is not a pattern yet, only an observation: the plan is not the point. The point is the person inside it — loose, unhurried, free to change their mind. A plan that cannot bend will be abandoned; a plan that bends gets used again.
