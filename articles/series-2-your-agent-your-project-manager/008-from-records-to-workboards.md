# Article 008 — From Records to Workboards

## The story

- **I had the records; I did not have the surface.** For months my work lived in records I already kept: a project tracker, a business ledger, a note-app booklist, an assignment list. Each one was accurate. None of them told me, at a glance, what to do next. To answer "what needs me today?" I had to open four files and hold the picture in my head.
- **One morning, four surfaces.** I asked my agent to build one work surface per job, each fed by the records I already kept: a project workboard for the moving pieces across my projects; a finance dashboard for the business, seeded from the ledger; a booklist for the degree; an assignment tracker for the term ahead. Four surfaces, one morning — because the data was already there, the build was assembly, not invention.
- **The first version was a report.** The finance dashboard showed balances and totals. It looked finished and was useless for the actual job, which was deciding what to pay, move, or check next. A report tells you what happened. A work surface tells you what to do next — and lets you do it there.
- **The fix came from one request.** I asked for one-tap status changes: tap a card and its status moves, right on the board. That request changed the build. Every surface after it was designed around the action first — what the person does on it — and the display second. The assignment tracker was built for future terms, not just this one, because the shape was right even before the data filled in.
- **Empty states had to be honest.** A surface with no data yet must say so plainly — "nothing here yet" — not show a cheerful zero or a fake sample. A board you cannot trust at a glance is a board you stop opening.

## In plain words

- **Workboard** — a living surface that shows a project's current state and lets you change it in place: move a card, flip a status, mark a task done — right on the board, without opening another file.
- **Dashboard** — a surface that gathers the numbers for one job — balances, budgets, totals — so you can see the whole picture at a glance. A dashboard becomes a workboard when you can act on it, not just read it.
- **Kanban** — a way of arranging work as cards in columns: each card is one item, each column is a stage. Move the card, and you move the work.

Every term also lives in the [glossary](../../glossary.md).

## How it works

The shape is: records → surface → action.

```text
The records you already keep
│
├── one surface per job — a workboard, a dashboard, a list
├── each surface fed by its own records, nothing invented
├── the action first — the tap, the move, the status change
└── honest empty states — "nothing here yet" beats a fake zero
```

The records are the truth (Pattern 1); the surface is the view; the action is the point. If a surface shows you something you cannot act on, it is decoration. If it needs data you do not already keep, it is a second job, and it will die.

Why one surface per job: a board that tries to do everything becomes the same pile the records already were. A project workboard, a finance dashboard, a booklist, and an assignment tracker look different from each other on purpose — you should know which job you are looking at before you read a word.

## Try it yourself

Build one workboard this week, the smallest version:

1. **Pick one job you already track.** Not a new system — one list, ledger, or tracker you already keep. If you do not keep the records yet, start there; a surface with nothing under it is a poster.
2. **Name the one action.** What do you do on this surface? Move a card to done? Mark a bill paid? Pick the next book? Design for that tap first.
3. **Build it from the records, not from scratch.** Every card, row, and number comes from something you already keep. Nothing typed in by hand twice.
4. **Make it visually distinct from your other surfaces.** Different shape, different emphasis — so you know the job at a glance.
5. **Leave the empty state honest.** If a section has nothing yet, say so. The day it fills in, the surface earns trust instead of suspicion.
6. **Use it for a week, then fix the one thing that annoys you.** The follow-up request — the one-tap change, the missing column — is part of the build, not a complaint about it.

## What it proved

Two patterns from [patterns.md](../../patterns.md), holding up in a second series:

- **Pattern 1 — Context is infrastructure you own.** The build was fast because the records already existed, in files I owned, in shapes my agent could read. A workboard built on records you own survives a change of tool; one built on a tool's private data does not.
- **Pattern 5 — Minimum viable complexity.** One surface per job, one action each, nothing extra. The moment a surface asked for data I did not already keep, it would have become a second job — and a second job is how a system dies.

And one thing that is not a pattern yet, only an observation: the surface is where the system meets the person. All the context, routing, and tracking in Series 1 only pays off if there is a place where the person can see it and touch it. Records are the memory; workboards are the hands.
